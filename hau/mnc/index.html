Host-country regulation

China’s FDI legal framework

Model 1

No special FDI law

General commercial law applies.

Model 2

No unified code

Rules scattered across sector statutes.

Model 3 · China

Unified FDI code

A dedicated Foreign Investment Law.

Foreign Investment Law

Adopted in 2019 and in force since 1 January 2020. It replaced the separate laws on equity joint ventures, cooperative joint ventures and wholly foreign-owned firms.

Negative list system

Sectors off the list get national treatment at entry. Listed sectors carry equity caps or bans, and car making was one until its caps were phased out.

Sources: Norton Rose Fulbright; China Daily
The policy under analysis

Lifting the auto equity cap

1994

Foreign carmakers capped at a 50% stake in any local venture.

2018

On 17 April the state planner announced a staged removal. The cap ended for electric and plug-in hybrid makers.

2020

The Chinese-ownership requirement lifted for commercial vehicles.

2022

Lifted for passenger cars. The limit of two joint ventures per foreign investor also ended.

Why it matters

Electric vehicles went first, so Tesla could build a wholly owned plant four years before the cap fell for passenger cars.

Sources: Reuters via WardsAuto; Norton Rose Fulbright
The case: Tesla Shanghai

How the policy toolkit was applied to Tesla

Sectors & equity

100% ownership. The cap was waived for EV makers, so Tesla Shanghai is not a joint venture.

Land & resources

864,885 m² in Lingang for RMB 973 million, under a contract signed 17 October 2018.

Taxes & incentives

15% income tax reported through 2023 versus the 25% standard, plus a purchase-tax exemption for EV buyers.

Screening & approval

Still licensed. Vehicle manufacturing needs a ministry licence even after the cap went.

Employment rules

Local hiring pledge. Reported commitment of one local hire per US$1.8 million of revenue.

State-bank finance

Up to RMB 11.25 billion (about US$1.6 billion) from four Chinese banks, December 2019.

Sources: China Briefing; Gasgoo; Electrek (SEC filing); Bloomberg Tax

The case: Tesla Shanghai

The bargain: what each side gave

China offered

  • A wholly owned plant, the first for a foreign carmaker
  • Land of 864,885 m² for RMB 973 million
  • A 15% income tax rate through 2023
  • Credit of up to RMB 11.25 billion from state banks

Tesla committed

  • At least RMB 14 billion (about US$2 billion) of capex by end-2023
  • Local hiring tied to revenue, as reported
  • About US$323 million a year in taxes, as reported
  • Local sourcing: about 30% of Model 3 parts by October 2019
Sources: Wikipedia, Gigafactory Shanghai; China Briefing; Gasgoo; Electrek

Analysis

Policy evaluation

Why it worked

  • Predictable. Dated, staged removal of the cap gave investors certainty.
  • Packaged. Equity, land, tax and credit arrived together, cutting time to market.
  • Mutual. China gained jobs, local suppliers, exports and a strong rival for domestic EV makers.

Limits and risks

  • Time-limited perks. The 15% tax rate ran through 2023.
  • Leased land. Tesla owns the factory but not the land.
  • Dependence. Licences, capex and hiring pledges bind Tesla, and US–China relations can shift the outlook.

Takeaways

Conclusion

1
Policy shapes entry mode

Removing the equity cap let Tesla choose full ownership over a joint venture.

2
Packages beat single tools

Equity, land, tax and finance together made the plant fast to build.

3
FDI policy is a bargain

The host gains jobs and exports, and the firm gains access, with commitments on both sides.

If the cap had stayed at 50%, would Tesla have built in Shanghai at all?
